# Due To vs Because Of

The traditional distinction between *due to* and *because of* is adjectival vs adverbial — a nuance most writers ignore, but one that still matters in formal and academic prose.

## Key Rules

1. **Due To (Adjectival)** — *Due to* modifies a noun. It follows a form of *to be* and answers *caused by what?*: *The cancellation was due to rain.* (cancellation = noun)
2. **Because Of (Adverbial)** — *Because of* modifies a verb. It answers *why?*: *The game was cancelled because of rain.* (cancelled = verb)
3. **The Test** — If you can replace *due to* with *caused by* and the sentence still works, you are using it correctly. If not, use *because of*.

✅ The delay was due to a software error. *(delay = noun; *caused by* works)*
❌ The flight was delayed due to a software error. *(delayed = verb; try *caused by* — it fails)*

✅ The flight was delayed because of a software error.
✅ The outage was due to a power failure at the data centre.

## Modern Usage Note

Most readers will not notice the distinction, and many style guides have relaxed the rule. In formal writing, however, preserving it signals grammatical sophistication. In casual or business writing, either form is generally accepted — though *because of* is almost never wrong.
