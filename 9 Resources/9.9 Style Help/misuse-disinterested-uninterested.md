# Disinterested vs Uninterested

These two words sound similar but mean different things. The distinction is under pressure from common usage, but careful writers preserve it — especially in legal, journalistic, and academic contexts where the difference matters.

## Key Rules

1. **Disinterested** — Impartial, unbiased, having no stake in the outcome. A judge should be disinterested.
2. **Uninterested** — Bored, indifferent, not caring. A student who stares at the clock is uninterested.
3. **Preserve the Distinction** — If you mean *impartial*, say *disinterested*. If you mean *bored*, say *uninterested*. No other pair captures this difference as cleanly.

✅ A disinterested third party mediated the dispute.
❌ A disinterested third party mediated the dispute. *(if you meant bored/uninvolved — incorrect)*

✅ She was uninterested in the lecture and spent the hour on her phone.
❌ She was disinterested in the lecture and spent the hour on her phone.

## Why It Matters

In journalism and law, calling a judge *uninterested* instead of *disinterested* changes the meaning entirely — from a compliment (fair) to an insult (checked out). When precision counts, use the right word.
