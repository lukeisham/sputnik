# Misuse of *Literally*

*Literally* means *in a literal sense; not figurative; actually*. Its use as an intensifier for figurative statements — *"I literally died laughing"* — is widespread in casual speech but undermines clear writing. The word exists to distinguish fact from metaphor; squandering it leaves no word to do that job.

## Key Rules

1. **Reserve for Literal Truth** — Use *literally* only when you mean something happened exactly as described, without metaphor or exaggeration.
2. **Avoid as an Intensifier** — *Literally amazing, literally the worst, literally exploded* — in all these, *literally* adds no information. It is verbal italics, not meaning.
3. **Test by Removing It** — If the sentence means the same thing without *literally*, delete it.

✅ The building literally collapsed at 3:14 p.m. — engineers confirmed the structure failed.
❌ The news literally collapsed my whole world. *(figurative — use figuratively or recast)*

✅ He ran the marathon in literally two hours and four minutes. *(factually accurate)*
❌ He literally flew through that marathon. *(unless he had wings)*

## A Note on Usage Drift

Some dictionaries now record the intensifier use as acceptable. In formal, academic, or professional writing, ignore this drift. The distinction is too useful to abandon.
