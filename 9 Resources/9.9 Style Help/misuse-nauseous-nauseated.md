# Nauseous vs Nauseated

The traditional distinction between *nauseous* and *nauseated* is fading from common use, but careful speakers and writers still preserve it. The question is simple: are you making someone sick, or are you feeling sick?

## Key Rules

1. **Nauseous** — Causing nausea. Something *nauseous* makes a person feel sick: *a nauseous smell, a nauseous display*. It describes the cause, not the feeling.
2. **Nauseated** — Feeling nausea. A person who is *nauseated* is experiencing the sensation of wanting to be sick.
3. **Nauseating** — Also means causing nausea; interchangeable with *nauseous* in the traditional sense.

✅ The nauseous fumes from the chemical spill forced an evacuation. *(the fumes cause sickness)*
✅ She felt nauseated after the boat ride. *(she is experiencing the feeling)*

❌ She felt nauseous after the boat ride. *(traditionalists object — she is not causing nausea)*
❌ The nauseated smell made everyone leave. *(smells do not feel nausea)*

## Usage Drift

In modern casual use, *I feel nauseous* is now so common that correcting it can seem pedantic. In formal writing, however — medical documentation, journalism, academic papers — the distinction remains valuable because it prevents ambiguity. When you need to be precise, use *nauseated* for the feeling and *nauseous* or *nauseating* for the cause.
